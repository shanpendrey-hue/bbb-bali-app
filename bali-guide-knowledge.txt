🟡 When should you travel to Bali?
Understanding Peak, Shoulder, and Quieter Travel Periods
Bali is a year-round destination, but the experience can vary significantly depending on when you visit. Tourism levels are influenced by global school holidays, major international travel periods, and Bali’s tropical climate. Understanding these travel seasons helps you choose the timing that best suits your preferences.
Peak Travel Periods
Peak periods generally coincide with times when large numbers of international and domestic travellers are able to travel.
These typically include:
International school holidays (particularly from Australia, Europe, and Southeast Asia)
Major global holiday windows such as Christmas, New Year, and Easter
Significant cultural festivals and events, including Galungan, Kuningan, and Nyepi (Balinese Day of Silence)
What to expect during peak periods:
Significantly higher airfares and accommodation rates
Heavier traffic, especially in areas such as Seminyak, Canggu, Ubud, and around the airport
Popular attractions, beaches, and restaurants operating at or near capacity
Advance bookings essential for villas, resorts, tours, and dining
Peak season offers vibrant energy and ideal beach weather, but it requires flexibility, patience, and a higher travel budget.
Shoulder Travel Periods
Shoulder periods sit between peak and quieter seasons and are often considered the most balanced time to visit Bali.
These periods typically offer:
Warm, pleasant weather with lower rainfall than the wet season
Noticeably fewer crowds compared to peak periods
Better availability of accommodation, tours, and dining
More competitive pricing without the extremes of low or high season
Why experienced travellers prefer shoulder season:
You can enjoy Bali’s landscapes, temples, and beaches without feeling rushed or overcrowded
Service levels often feel more personal
It is easier to move between regions and explore at a relaxed pace
Shoulder seasons are ideal for travellers seeking a mix of good weather, cultural exploration, and comfort without peak-season pressure.
Quieter (Low) Travel Periods
Quieter periods generally align with Bali’s wetter months, when rainfall and humidity are higher.
During quieter periods, you may experience:
Fewer international tourists and a calmer atmosphere
Attractive accommodation deals, including luxury villas at reduced rates
Short, intense rain showers rather than constant rain
Lusher landscapes, rice fields, and waterfalls at their most vibrant
While beach conditions may be less predictable, this season can be excellent for:
Cultural experiences
Wellness retreats and yoga stays
Budget-conscious or slow-travel visitors
Travellers who value space and tranquility over perfect weather
Best Time to Visit Bali: A Balanced Perspective
There is no single “perfect” time to visit Bali.
The best time for you depends on:
Your tolerance for crowds and traffic
Your flexibility with tropical weather patterns
Your main priorities, such as beach time, cultural experiences, nature, or relaxation
Your travel budget and accommodation expectations
Rather than aiming for an idealised version of Bali, it is best to choose your travel dates based on what matters most to you. Each season offers a different side of the island, and Bali rewards travellers who approach it with openness and adaptability.

🔴 Understanding Entry Requirements
Entry requirements for Bali (Indonesia) vary depending on:
Your nationality and passport type
The purpose of your visit (tourism, business, transit, long-stay)
Length of stay
Current Indonesian government policies
These requirements can change with little notice due to policy updates, security measures, or public health considerations. Travellers should always confirm the latest information before departure.
Visa Options (High-Level Overview)
Most travellers entering Bali will require some form of visa or entry permission, even for short tourist stays.
Common visa pathways may include:
Short-stay tourist visas
Visa on arrival or electronic visa options (where applicable)
Pre-arranged visas obtained before travel
Longer-stay visas for extended travel, business, or special purposes
Visa availability, duration, extension rules, and eligibility differ by nationality and visa type. What applies to one traveller may not apply to another.
Passport Requirements
In addition to holding a valid visa (where required), travellers are generally expected to:
Hold a passport valid for a minimum period beyond their intended stay
Have at least one or more blank passport pages
Ensure their passport is in good condition (no damage, missing pages, or excessive wear)
Failure to meet passport requirements may result in denied boarding or refusal of entry.
Additional Entry Requirements
Depending on current regulations, travellers may also be required to provide:
Proof of onward or return travel
Evidence of sufficient funds for the duration of stay
Completion of arrival or customs declarations (often online)
Health, insurance, or vaccination documentation (when applicable)
Requirements can differ based on arrival method, length of stay, or recent travel history.
Important Notes for Travellers
Entry rules can change rapidly and without extensive public notice
Airlines may enforce entry requirements before allowing boarding
Travellers are responsible for ensuring they meet all entry conditions prior to departure
Relying on outdated information can result in delays, fines, or denied entry

🟡 Spending in Bali: How Much Should You Budget Per Day?
One of the most common questions people ask before travelling to Bali is:
“How much should I expect to spend each day?”
The honest answer is — it depends on the type of holiday you want.
Is Bali Still Affordable?
You’ll often hear people say that “Bali isn’t cheap anymore.”
While this can be true in certain pockets, it’s not the full picture.
Bali hasn’t become unaffordable — it has simply become more diverse.
You can spend very little, a moderate amount, or indulge in luxury — sometimes all within the same day. Your daily budget will largely depend on:
Where you stay
Which areas you visit
Where you eat and drink
How you get around
The experiences you choose
Location Matters
Costs can vary significantly depending on the suburb or area you’re in.
Popular, highly frequented areas with well-known cafés, beach clubs, and restaurants naturally come with higher price tags — especially in prime locations or peak seasons. These areas are designed for convenience, atmosphere, and accessibility, and you’ll often pay a premium for that.
That said, higher prices don’t automatically mean better experiences.
Just a short distance away from the most talked-about hotspots, you’ll often find:
Excellent food
Safe, welcoming environments
Great service
A relaxed, authentic vibe
Significantly more affordable pricing
Some of the best meals, cafés, and experiences in Bali are found in tourist areas — just not always in the most heavily marketed venues.
Accommodation, Dining & Daily Costs
Where you stay and how you dine will have the biggest impact on your daily spend.
Accommodation ranges from simple guesthouses to luxury villas and resorts
Dining can be anything from local warungs to high-end international restaurants
Transport and activities can be tailored to suit almost any budget
Bali allows you to choose your comfort level without sacrificing safety or enjoyment.
Affordable Doesn’t Mean Unsafe
A common misconception is that cheaper options are less safe or lower quality. In Bali, this simply isn’t true.
With a little research:
Affordable accommodation can be clean, comfortable, and well-located
Local eateries can be some of the safest and most enjoyable places to eat
Less “hyped” areas can offer better value and a more relaxed experience
Doing some basic research into neighbourhoods and reading current reviews goes a long way.
The Bottom Line
Bali is what you make it.
If you want a high-end, luxury holiday — Bali can deliver that.
If you’re travelling mindfully and looking for value — Bali can absolutely deliver that too.
Understanding where to go, where to spend, and where to save allows you to shape a holiday that suits your budget, your comfort level, and your travel style.
And often, it’s the balance between the two that makes the experience truly special.

🟡 Food Intolerances in Bali
Bali is generally very accommodating when it comes to food intolerances and dietary requirements. Many restaurants — particularly in popular tourist areas — are familiar with common needs such as gluten-free, dairy-free, vegetarian, vegan, and nut-free options.
That said, how well your needs can be accommodated will depend on:
The type of restaurant
The area you’re dining in
The complexity of your intolerance
Do Your Research First
If you have food intolerances or allergies, it’s always a good idea to:
Check menus online before you go
Look for restaurants that clearly list dietary options
Read recent reviews from people with similar requirements
Many venues are happy to cater — but preparation helps reduce risk.
Ask Questions (and Trust Your Instincts)
Don’t be afraid to ask questions or clarify ingredients when ordering. Most staff are friendly and willing to help.
However, it’s important to be mindful that:
A “yes” doesn’t always guarantee full understanding
Language barriers can sometimes lead to miscommunication
Cross-contamination may not always be fully avoidable
If something doesn’t feel clear or you’re unsure, it’s okay to choose another option.
Keep It Simple
When managing intolerances:
Simpler dishes are often safer
Avoid assuming substitutions are automatically safe
Clearly explain what you cannot eat, not just what you prefer
Playing it safe is always better than risking discomfort or illness — just as it would be anywhere in the world.
🟡 What to Pack (Practical, Not Excessive)
Depending on your trip, your requirements may vary.
However, for the majority of travellers visiting Bali for a relaxed leisure holiday, the guidance below covers what you are most likely to need.
Bali’s climate, lifestyle, and infrastructure mean that packing thoughtfully — rather than heavily — will make your trip more comfortable and flexible.
Clothing
Bali has a warm, tropical climate year-round, with high humidity. Clothing that is lightweight and breathable will be the most comfortable, particularly if you plan to spend time outdoors or moving between locations.
Most travellers should pack:
Lightweight summer clothing suitable for warm days
Loose, breathable fabrics such as cotton, linen, or moisture-wicking blends
Swimwear for beaches, pools, and resorts
A light layer (such as a cardigan, hoodie, or scarf) for flights, evenings, or air-conditioned spaces like restaurants and shopping centres
Heavy or cold-weather clothing is generally unnecessary, even in the evenings.
If you plan to visit temples or attend cultural experiences, modest clothing is important. While sarongs are often available at temple entrances, having outfits that cover shoulders and knees can be convenient.
Footwear
Footwear requirements in Bali depend largely on your activities and where you plan to dine or spend time.
Casual footwear
Sandals or comfortable walking shoes are suitable for daily wear
Flip-flops (thongs) are common for beaches and casual outings
Dining and venues
Some restaurants, beach clubs, and bars — particularly in Seminyak, Canggu, and Ubud — may require:
Closed-toe shoes
Shirts for men
No swimwear or beachwear during dining hours
Bringing at least one pair of closed shoes allows flexibility if you plan to visit higher-end venues or dine out in the evenings.
Activities and excursions
If you plan on:
Walking tours
Hiking or cycling
Adventure activities such as rafting or ATV riding
It’s advisable to bring runners or sports shoes. While protective gear is often provided for organised activities, appropriate footwear is not always included or available in your size.
Bags & Daily Carry
A practical day bag can make getting around much easier.
Useful options include:
A small cross-body bag for daily outings
A lightweight backpack for longer days, tours, or shopping
Bags that zip securely and sit close to the body are ideal for busy areas
If you forget to pack a suitable bag, these are easy and affordable to purchase locally across Bali.
Shopping in Bali: Packing Flexibility
Bali is a very easy place to shop if you arrive under-prepared.
If you need to pick things up after arrival:
Street shops and local markets offer affordable clothing, footwear, and accessories
Shopping centres and boutiques carry international brands, often priced similarly to home
Many shopping centres and retail areas stay open late into the evening
Because of this, many travellers prefer to pack lighter and purchase additional items once they arrive.
A Practical Approach to Packing
Bali rewards flexibility. You don’t need to bring everything with you, and you don’t need to dress for every scenario in advance. Pack for comfort, versatility, and the activities you know you’ll enjoy — and adjust once you arrive.
🟡 Taking Your Electronics to Bali
Travelling with electronics is a normal part of modern travel — and Bali is no exception. Whether you’re bringing a phone, laptop, camera, tablet, or multiple devices, a small amount of preparation will help ensure everything works smoothly and safely once you arrive.
Power & Plug Types
Bali uses Type C and Type F plugs, which are the two-pin round plugs commonly used throughout much of Europe.
Electricity standards:
Voltage: 230V
Frequency: 50Hz
Most modern electronics — including phones, laptops, tablets, cameras, and power banks — are dual-voltage and designed to work internationally. You can usually confirm this by checking your charger or power adapter, which should state “100–240V”.
Many hotels, villas, and resorts — particularly mid-range to higher-end accommodations — offer universal power outlets that accept multiple plug types. Depending on your home country, you may find that your devices plug in without an adapter at all.
However, outlet availability varies, and not all rooms or properties offer universal sockets.
Best practice:
Always pack a power adapter to be safe, especially if you’re relying on your devices for navigation, communication, or work.
Forget Your Adapter? You Have Options
If you forget to pack a power adapter, it’s usually easy to solve once you arrive.
Many accommodations can loan a power adapter to guests (availability varies)
Power adapters are widely sold:
At airports
In convenience stores
In street shops and markets
At shopping centres and electronics stores
Adapters are inexpensive and easy to find across Bali, so forgetting one is rarely a trip-disrupting issue.
Power Stability & Surge Protection
Power supply in Bali is generally reliable, but brief outages or voltage fluctuations can occur — particularly during storms or in more rural areas.
To protect your electronics:
Use a surge-protected power board if you’re charging multiple devices
Avoid leaving devices plugged in during heavy storms
Unplug sensitive equipment when not in use
This is especially important for high-value items such as laptops, cameras, drones, and external hard drives.
Airport & Customs Considerations
Indonesia has specific regulations around electronics, particularly mobile phones.
Personal electronics for personal use are generally permitted
If you plan to use a local SIM card for an extended stay, your phone may need to be registered (IMEI registration)
Short-term visitors typically do not encounter issues, but rules and enforcement can change
For the most up-to-date requirements, always refer to official government or airport guidance before travel.
Using Electronics in a Tropical Climate
Bali’s tropical climate is warm and humid, which can affect electronics over time if precautions aren’t taken.
Helpful tips include:
Avoid leaving devices in direct sunlight
Use dry bags or protective cases when near water
Allow devices to acclimate when moving between air-conditioned rooms and outdoor heat
Consider using silica gel packets in camera or tech bags to reduce moisture buildup
Humidity is one of the most common causes of long-term electronic damage in tropical environments, particularly for cameras and laptops.
A Practical Approach
Between universal outlets, readily available adapters, and easily accessible electronics stores, Bali is well-equipped for modern travellers. Packing thoughtfully — and knowing that solutions are readily available — helps reduce stress and keeps your focus on enjoying your trip.

🟡 VPNs & Digital Security
VPNs & Digital Security While Travelling
What Is a VPN?
A VPN (Virtual Private Network) creates a secure, encrypted connection between your device and the internet. Instead of your data being transmitted openly across a network, a VPN encrypts it, making it far more difficult for third parties to intercept, monitor, or exploit your information.
In practical terms, a VPN acts as a protective tunnel for your online activity. It helps safeguard sensitive data such as passwords, financial details, and personal communications, regardless of where you are accessing the internet.
Why Digital Security Matters When Travelling
When travelling, people rely heavily on public or shared Wi-Fi networks. These networks are designed for convenience, not security, and are commonly found in:
Airports and airline lounges
Hotels and resorts
Cafés and restaurants
Co-working spaces
Shopping centres and transport hubs
Public Wi-Fi networks are often unencrypted or poorly secured, which means other users on the same network — or malicious actors nearby — may be able to intercept data being transmitted. This risk exists anywhere in the world, including in highly developed countries.
Common Risks of Unsecured Wi-Fi
Without proper protection, public networks can expose travellers to:
Password theft through data interception
Access to personal or work email accounts
Compromised banking or payment information
Identity theft through stolen personal details
Account takeovers on booking platforms, social media, or cloud services
Malware installation through fake or compromised networks
These risks are not limited to travel — they also apply in everyday life — but they increase significantly when users are moving between unfamiliar networks and locations.
How a VPN Reduces These Risks
Using a VPN significantly reduces exposure by:
Encrypting all internet traffic so data cannot be easily read or intercepted
Protecting login credentials when accessing sensitive accounts
Securing communication between apps and websites
Preventing tracking on open or shared networks
Reducing the risk of “man-in-the-middle” attacks on public Wi-Fi
A VPN is especially important when:
Accessing banking or payment apps
Logging into email or cloud storage
Managing travel bookings or itineraries
Entering passport, address, or personal information
Working remotely or accessing company systems
Using unfamiliar or unsecured Wi-Fi networks
VPN Use: A Travel Essential and Everyday Habit
While VPN use is strongly recommended during travel, it is increasingly considered a smart practice in everyday digital life as well. Many people use VPNs routinely at home, at work, or on mobile devices to maintain privacy, protect data, and reduce online exposure.
For travellers, a VPN should be treated as a basic digital safety tool, alongside travel insurance, secure backups, and document protection.
Staying Informed and Choosing a VPN
Not all VPN providers offer the same level of security, privacy standards, or reliability. Travellers should:
Choose reputable, well-reviewed providers
Avoid free VPNs that may collect or sell user data
Ensure the VPN supports strong encryption and a no-logs policy
Install and test the VPN before departure

Reference Guide: 🟡 🟢
Bali is often misunderstood due to headlines, social media, and second-hand stories that lack context. While Bali — like anywhere in the world — has risks, it is generally safe, well-regulated in tourist areas, and deeply rooted in cultural, spiritual, and community values.

🟡 Volcano Activity & Flight Delays
Bali sits within the Pacific Ring of Fire, meaning volcanic activity is a natural and well-monitored part of life. The most active volcanoes near tourist areas are Mount Agung and Mount Batur. When these volcanoes release volcanic ash plumes, it does not automatically mean danger for people on the ground.
Flight delays or cancellations occur because volcanic ash can damage aircraft engines and reduce visibility, making flying unsafe. This is an aviation safety decision — not a reflection of danger within Bali itself. Authorities closely monitor volcanic activity and issue alerts well in advance if any action is required.
What to know:
Volcanic activity is monitored 24/7 by Indonesian authorities
Ash does not equal lava flow or evacuation risk
Flight delays are precautionary, not emergency-based
Ground safety is rarely affected
Always monitor your airline directly for updates
🟡 Earthquakes & Other Natural Disasters in Bali
Bali is located in a geologically active region, meaning that earthquakes and other natural events can occur. This can sound alarming to travellers unfamiliar with the area, but it’s important to understand the context, frequency, and reality of these events so they’re not misunderstood or overstated.
Indonesia sits along the Pacific Ring of Fire, a region known for seismic and volcanic activity. As a result, small earthquakes are relatively common, while significant, damaging earthquakes are far less frequent — especially in Bali’s main tourist areas.
Understanding Earthquakes in Bali
Most earthquakes felt in Bali are:
Low magnitude
Short in duration
Non-damaging
Felt as mild shaking rather than violent movement
Many are offshore or deep underground and are often more noticeable because buildings are lightweight, not because the earthquake itself is severe. In many cases, locals barely react — a good indicator of the actual risk level.
Indonesia has active monitoring systems in place, and authorities issue alerts and guidance when needed.
🟡 Tsunamis & Major Seismic Events
While earthquakes can raise concerns about tsunamis, the risk in Bali is very low, particularly compared to other parts of Indonesia.
Bali’s geography and reef systems reduce tsunami risk
Tsunami warnings are rare and would be communicated clearly if issued
Tourist areas have evacuation signage in coastal zones
Significant seismic events are extremely uncommon, and emergency procedures are in place should they occur.
🟡 Other Natural Events to Be Aware Of
In addition to earthquakes, Bali can experience:
Heavy rain and flooding, particularly during rainy season
Strong winds or storms, usually short-lived
Occasional power outages related to weather
These events are usually localised and temporary, and life quickly returns to normal.
🟡 What to Do if You Experience an Earthquake
Most earthquakes pass quickly and require no action beyond staying calm.
General guidance:
Stay calm and avoid panic
Move away from windows, shelves, or unsecured objects
If indoors, stay inside and shelter near a solid structure
If outdoors, move to an open area away from buildings and power lines
Follow instructions from accommodation staff or local authorities
Hotels and villas in tourist areas are generally built with seismic awareness in mind and staff are trained to respond appropriately.
🟡 Reassurance for Travellers
Earthquakes are a part of life in many regions around the world, including places people travel to every day. In Bali, they are:
Closely monitored
Rarely disruptive
Very unlikely to impact your trip in a serious way
Understanding the environment helps remove fear. Being aware — not anxious — is the best approach.

This is reflected in daily offerings (canang sari) placed outside homes and businesses as a reminder of respect and moral responsibility.
Most issues visitors experience are due to miscommunication, not malicious intent. Being clear, calm, and confident significantly reduces risk.

Trust your instincts

Bali does have a visible population of street dogs and cats, particularly in some neighbourhoods and coastal areas. That said, even friendly-looking animals can behave unpredictably, particularly if they’re protecting territory, are unwell, are feeding, or are startled. The key message isn’t to be fearful — it’s to be sensible. For most travellers, avoiding direct contact prevents nearly all risk.

🟡 Money Exchange Safety

The best way to avoid problems is to exchange money at reputable, established money changers and to stay fully present while the transaction is happening. Most issues occur when people are distracted, rushed, or accept an exchange without verifying the final amount. With a few simple habits, exchanging money in Bali can be very safe and easy.
Safe exchange tips:
Use established money changers (clean, professional storefronts) rather than temporary stalls

Watch the money being counted in front of you, and count it yourself before leaving the counter
Confirm the exchange rate and final amount before handing over your cash
Request a printed receipt and keep it with you
Don’t feel pressured or rushed — if anything feels off, politely cancel and walk away

🟡 Tap Water Safety
In Bali, tap water is generally not considered safe for drinking due to infrastructure and treatment differences — this is common across many parts of Southeast Asia. Most travellers are fine using tap water for showering and handwashing, but drinking it can increase the risk of stomach upset, especially for sensitive travellers. The good news is that bottled water and filtered water are widely available, and most accommodation is set up for tourists with safe drinking options.
Best practice:
Drink bottled or filtered water only
If you have a sensitive stomach, use filtered water for brushing teeth as well
Avoid swallowing water in the shower
Be mindful of ice — most reputable venues use safe ice, but if you’re unsure, ask
Hydrate more than you think — heat and humidity increase dehydration risk
🟡 Bali Belly Concerns
Bali Belly is talked about so often that people assume it’s guaranteed — it isn’t. Many travellers never experience it. Reduce risk by:
Washing hands regularly and using hand sanitiser when needed
Drinking safe water only, and staying consistently hydrated
Choosing reputable food venues and starting simple if you’re sensitive
Avoiding food that has been sitting uncovered for long periods
Carrying basic stomach support items in your travel kit (as advised by your pharmacist/doctor)
Seeking medical advice early if symptoms become severe or persist
🟡 “Bali Belly Comes From Local Food”

🟢 Everything Runs on ‘Island Time

Bali rewards a balanced approach: embracing flexibility where it exists, while respecting the systems and schedules that keep things running smoothly.
🟡 You Can Ignore Cultural Etiquette

Visiting Bali is no different to entering any other country. Just as you would be expected to respect laws, customs, and social norms in your own country, the same courtesy applies here.
Ignoring cultural etiquette isn’t harmless, even if no one confronts you. It can be deeply disrespectful, particularly in sacred spaces or during ceremonies. Taking a moment to observe, ask questions, and follow local guidance shows respect — and is a simple way to honour the culture that welcomes you.

🟡 Eating in Bali (Safely & Comfortably)
Bali has a diverse and vibrant food scene, ranging from small local warungs and street vendors to cafés, beach clubs, and high-end international restaurants. Most travellers eat well throughout their trip — but how and where you eat can make a big difference to your comfort, particularly if you have a sensitive stomach or are travelling for the first time.
Food safety in Bali is less about avoiding local food altogether, and more about making informed choices.
Restaurants vs Street Food
Bali offers everything from polished international restaurants to small roadside food stalls, and both can be enjoyable. The key difference is consistency and predictability, especially for travellers whose stomachs are not used to local food or water.
Restaurants & Cafés
Restaurants and cafés that cater to international travellers generally:
Follow higher and more consistent hygiene and food-handling standards
Use treated or filtered water in food preparation
Are familiar with food intolerances and dietary requests
Turn over ingredients quickly due to steady customer volume
Because of this, restaurants and cafés are usually the lowest-risk option, particularly:
At the beginning of your trip
While your body is adjusting to a new environment
If you have a sensitive stomach or dietary restrictions
This doesn’t mean these venues are “better” — just more predictable.
Street Food & Roadside Vendors
Street food in Bali can be:
Delicious and culturally important
Freshly cooked and enjoyable
Completely safe in many cases
However, it may not be suitable for everyone, particularly:
Travellers with sensitive stomachs
Those not used to local bacteria or food handling methods
People early in their trip before their body has adjusted
Street food isn’t automatically unsafe — but it is more variable.
The increased risk usually comes from:
Water quality used for washing ingredients or utensils
Food being prepared earlier and sitting out
Limited or inconsistent refrigeration
Preparation methods that differ from what your body is used to
Food can be safe one day and not the next depending on how it’s handled, stored, or prepared.
This is why some travellers eat street food without issue — and others don’t.
How to Choose Where to Eat (Smartly)
Regardless of whether you’re eating at a restaurant or a street stall:
Read recent reviews, not just overall ratings
Look for comments about cleanliness, food quality, and consistency
Observe the venue:
Is it clean?
Are staff handling food carefully?
Are ingredients stored properly?
Busy venues with high customer turnover are often safer than quiet ones, as food is prepared and replaced frequently.
Price alone is not a reliable indicator of safety.
Expensive doesn’t always mean safer — and affordable doesn’t mean risky.
Trust Your Instincts
If a place:
Smells off
Looks poorly maintained

🟡 Bali Belly
What is Bali Belly?
“Bali Belly” is a common term used to describe traveller’s diarrhoea or gastrointestinal illness experienced by some visitors to Bali. It is usually caused by exposure to unfamiliar bacteria, viruses, or parasites that the body is not used to.
While most cases are mild, symptoms can escalate quickly if left untreated.
Common Symptoms
Symptoms can appear suddenly and may include:
Diarrhoea (mild to severe)
Stomach cramps or abdominal pain
Nausea or vomiting
Bloating
Fever
Fatigue or weakness
Dehydration
How Bali Belly Can Happen
Bali Belly is not just caused by food or drinking water.
It can occur through:
Consuming contaminated food or drinks
Ice made from untreated water
Poor hand hygiene
Touching contaminated surfaces and then:
Putting your hands in your mouth
Touching your face, lips, or eyes
Using shared items (menus, door handles, money) without washing hands
Brushing teeth with tap water (for sensitive stomachs)
Even travellers who are careful with what they eat can still get Bali Belly through simple everyday contact.
What to Do if You Feel Symptoms
The moment you start to feel symptoms, it is strongly recommended to contact a well-renowned doctor in Bali for professional medical advice and treatment.
Many reputable doctors and medical services in Bali:
Are contactable via WhatsApp
Provide on-call villa and hotel visits
Can usually attend within 3–4 hours or less, depending on location, traffic, and patient demand
Early treatment can:
Reduce symptom severity
Shorten recovery time
Prevent dehydration or complications
Do not wait until symptoms worsen.

🟡 Mosquitoes & Health Risks
Mosquitoes are common in Bali, especially at dawn and dusk, around gardens, rice fields, still water, and during humid or rainy periods. Most bites are simply annoying — but mosquitoes can carry illness in tropical regions, so consistent prevention is worth the effort. The good news is that basic precautions are highly effective, and most travellers do not experience anything beyond mild bites. The goal is to reduce exposure, particularly in the early evening, and to treat bites early so you don’t end up with irritated, infected skin from scratching.
Reduce risk by:
Applying insect repellent daily, especially in the late afternoon and evenings
Wearing light, breathable long sleeves or loose pants at dusk if you’re prone to bites
Using mosquito coils, plug-ins, or sprays in your room if recommended by your accommodation
Keeping doors closed and using nets/screens where available
Avoiding scratching bites — this is one of the biggest causes of secondary infection
Seeking medical advice if you develop fever, body aches, rash, or worsening symptoms
🟡 Tomcat Beetles (Often Called “Bites”)
Tomcat beetles are often blamed for “bites,” but they don’t bite or sting. The irritation happens when the beetle is accidentally brushed or crushed against the skin, releasing a toxin that can cause a burning rash and sometimes blister-like marks. This can look dramatic and is understandably alarming if you’ve never seen it before. The most important thing is to avoid crushing the insect on your skin and to treat the area quickly if contact happens. Most cases resolve with basic care, but if the rash spreads or becomes painful, medical advice is recommended.
If contact occurs:
Wash the area immediately with soap and cool water
Do not rub the skin (rubbing can spread the toxin)
Avoid scratching, and keep the area clean and dry
Use a soothing cream if needed (pharmacies can advise)
Seek medical advice if blistering worsens, the rash spreads, or it affects the face/eyes
🟡 Other Common Insect Bites
In Bali, it’s normal to encounter bites from sandflies, midges, ants, and other small insects — particularly near beaches, vegetation, and outdoor dining areas. Most bites are mild and resolve quickly, but some people react more strongly depending on skin sensitivity. Scratching is the main cause of complications because it can break the skin and lead to infection. The best approach is prevention, quick treatment, and keeping bites clean.
Management tips:
Use repellent consistently, not just when you “notice mosquitoes”
Treat bites early with soothing creams or antihistamines if you react strongly
Keep nails short to reduce skin damage if you scratch unconsciously
If a bite becomes hot, swollen, painful, or shows signs of infection, seek medical advice
If you have a history of allergies, carry your usual medications and don’t delay treatment

🟢 The Sun in Bali: Understanding UV Exposure
Bali sits close to the equator, which means the sun here is strong year-round — often much stronger than people expect, even on cloudy days.
Many travellers underestimate Bali’s UV levels, especially if they’re used to cooler climates or assume that cloud cover offers protection. In reality, UV rays penetrate through clouds, and sun exposure can build quickly without you realising it.
Why Bali’s UV Is Different
Because of its location near the equator:
UV levels are consistently high throughout the year
The sun is more direct, especially between 10am and 3pm
You can burn faster than expected, even with short exposure
It’s common for people to get sunburned within their first few days simply because they don’t feel how intense the sun actually is.
Cloudy Days Still Count
One of the biggest misconceptions is that overcast or hazy days are “safe” sun days.
In Bali:
You can still experience high UV exposure on cloudy days
Humidity and heat can mask the sensation of burning
Reflection from sand, water, and concrete increases exposure
If you’re outdoors, sun protection is still important — regardless of how the sky looks.
Sun Protection Essentials
To protect yourself from Bali’s UV rays:
Wear broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30–50+)
Reapply every 2 hours, and after swimming or sweating
Use hats, sunglasses, and lightweight long-sleeve clothing
Seek shade during peak UV hours where possible
UV damage isn’t just about sunburn — it contributes to dehydration, fatigue, and long-term skin damage.
Take It Easy in Your First Few Days
If you’ve just arrived, give your body time to adjust:
Limit long sun exposure initially
Avoid full-day sun activities back-to-back
Stay hydrated and take breaks in shaded or air-conditioned areas
Easing into Bali’s climate helps prevent burnout and allows you to enjoy your trip more comfortably.
The sun in Bali is beautiful — but powerful.
Being mindful of UV exposure doesn’t mean staying indoors or avoiding the outdoors. It simply means respecting the climate and taking small, consistent steps to protect yourself.
When you do, you’ll feel better, recover faster, and be able to enjoy everything Bali has to offer without unnecessary discomfort.
Swimming is a big part of the Bali experience — whether you’re relaxing in a villa pool, enjoying a hotel resort, or heading to the ocean. Understanding the differences between pool swimming and ocean swimming in Bali will help you stay safe and enjoy the water with confidence.
🟢 Hotel & Villa Pools
Swimming Pools in Bali: What to Expect & How to Stay Comfortable
Swimming pools are widely available across Bali and are often one of the highlights of where you stay — from boutique hotels and private villas to larger resorts. Thanks to the warm climate, pools are used frequently and are an important part of the accommodation experience.
Pool Standards & Maintenance
Most hotels, villas, and resorts in Bali maintain their pools through:
Regular cleaning and filtration
Chemical treatment (such as chlorine or salt systems)
Routine checks by maintenance staff or pool technicians
In well-managed properties, pools are monitored frequently, particularly in places with high guest turnover. However, as with accommodation anywhere in the world, standards can vary depending on:
The type of accommodation (villa, guesthouse, resort)
The location
How often the pool is used
The size of the property and maintenance schedule
Before swimming, take a moment to look at the pool:
Water should appear clear
Surfaces should look clean
There should be no strong or unusual odour
If a pool looks cloudy, has visible debris, or smells strongly of chemicals, it’s best to avoid swimming and let staff know so they can address it.
Open-Air Pools & Natural Conditions
Most pools in Bali are open-air, which is part of their appeal but also means:
Leaves, insects, or small debris can appear — this is normal in tropical environments
Pools may warm quickly in the sun, especially smaller or shallow pools
Rain can temporarily affect water clarity or surfaces
Many villas and resorts now use saltwater pools, which:
Still use filtration and sanitisation
Are often gentler on skin, eyes, and hair
Can feel more comfortable for frequent swimming
Safety & Practical Considerations
Pool surrounds can become slippery, especially after rain
Steps and edges may not always be clearly marked
Depths can change quickly in infinity or resort-style pools
If you’re travelling with children:
Check whether the pool is fenced or has safety gates
Never assume supervision is provided
Always supervise children closely, even in shallow pools
Health & Hygiene Awareness
The same pool hygiene principles apply in Bali as they do anywhere in the world.
To reduce risk:
Avoid swimming with open cuts, grazes, or skin infections
Try not to swallow pool water
Be mindful of getting water in ears, particularly if you’re prone to ear infections
Shower before and after swimming to remove sunscreen, sweat, and chlorine
Pools are generally safe, but extended time in warm water — especially in hot weather — can increase dehydration without you realising it.
Staying Comfortable in the Heat
Drink water regularly while spending time by the pool
Take breaks from direct sun
Use shade during the hottest parts of the day
Reapply sunscreen after swimming
A Balanced Perspective
Swimming pools in Bali are no more risky than pools anywhere else in the world. Most issues arise not from the pool itself, but from:
Heat and dehydration
Overexposure to sun
Minor hygiene oversights
A quick visual check, basic awareness, and common sense go a long way.
Pools are a major part of the Bali experience and are generally well maintained. With simple checks, basic hygiene habits, and awareness of tropical conditions, you can enjoy pool time comfortably and safely — just as you would anywhere else.
🟢 Swimming in the Ocean
Bali’s beaches are beautiful, but ocean swimming here requires more awareness than many travellers expect.
Ocean Conditions in Bali
The ocean around Bali can vary greatly depending on:
Location
Time of year
Tides and weather conditions
Some beaches are calm and suitable for swimming, while others have:
Strong currents
Large waves
Sudden drop-offs
Conditions can change quickly, even on days that appear calm.
Rip Currents & Safety
Rip currents are present on many Bali beaches and are one of the main ocean risks.
Always swim between the red and yellow flags where available
If no flags are present, observe locals and ask before entering the water
Avoid swimming alone or at unfamiliar beaches
If caught in a rip current:
Stay calm
Do not swim directly against it
Float and signal for help, or swim parallel to the shore
Local Knowledge Matters
Certain beaches in Bali are better known for surfing rather than swimming.
Just because others are in the water doesn’t mean it’s safe to swim
Surf beaches often have stronger currents and reef breaks
Local advice is invaluable — when in doubt, ask
Marine Life & Environmental Awareness
Sea urchins, coral, and reef cuts can occur in some areas
Wearing reef shoes can be helpful in rocky or coral-heavy locations
Always be mindful of marine life and avoid touching coral
Weather, Tides & Timing
High tide and low tide can drastically change swimming conditions
After heavy rain, water quality may be reduced near river outlets
Early mornings are often calmer and clearer
Swimming in Bali can be incredibly enjoyable when done with awareness.
Pools offer a relaxed, controlled environment, while the ocean provides beauty, energy, and connection to nature — but demands respect. Taking a few precautions allows you to enjoy both safely and comfortably throughout your stay.
🟢 Ocean Animals in Bali
For the vast majority of travellers, negative encounters with ocean animals in Bali are rare. Most concerns come from unfamiliarity with tropical marine environments rather than actual risk.
That said, Bali’s waters are home to marine life, and a small number of species are worth being aware of — particularly if you are swimming, surfing, snorkelling, or spending extended time in the ocean.
The most commonly encountered ocean-related issue for travellers is jellyfish, which can appear seasonally depending on beach location, weather patterns, and currents. Most stings are mild, but some can be painful and require medical attention.
Other marine animals are generally not aggressive toward humans and do not seek interaction. Incidents typically occur when people:
accidentally make contact
ignore local warnings
swim in unsuitable conditions
Awareness and caution — not fear — are what matter.
What to Do in the Ocean
Follow local beach signage, flag systems, and lifeguard instructions
Ask locals or staff about current ocean conditions before swimming
Avoid swimming if jellyfish warnings are posted
Do not touch marine life, even if it appears harmless or inactive
Wear protective swimwear if swimming during jellyfish seasons
Avoid swimming alone, especially at unfamiliar beaches
If You Are Stung or Injured
Leave the water calmly and safely
Do not rub the affected area
Seek medical attention promptly
Follow first-aid guidance provided by lifeguards or medical staff
Refer to the Live Updates page for current medical guidance and recommended facilities
Bali’s ocean is one of its greatest highlights. Travellers who respect local advice, understand conditions, and take simple precautions generally enjoy the water safely and confidently.
🟢 Showering, Pool Water & How It Can Affect Your Skin and Hair
Many travellers notice changes to their skin or hair while in Bali. This is common and usually temporary. The reasons are not unique to Bali — they’re the result of different water treatment, climate, and increased exposure to sun, heat, and swimming, just as they would be in many destinations around the world.
Shower Water in Bali
Most accommodation in Bali uses treated water for showering, bathing, and washing. While it’s safe for external use, the mineral content, treatment methods, and water source can differ from what your body is used to at home.
These differences can sometimes affect:
Hair texture and moisture
Scalp sensitivity
Skin hydration and balance
How Hair Can Be Affected
Your hair’s response will depend on factors such as:
Hair type (fine, thick, curly, straight)
Colour treatment (bleached, highlighted, dyed)
Scalp sensitivity
Frequency of swimming
Common changes travellers notice include:
Hair feeling drier or more brittle
Increased frizz, especially in humidity
Colour-treated or blonde hair appearing dull, brassy, or slightly discoloured
Scalp dryness or itchiness
Faster oil build-up in humid conditions
These effects are usually caused by:
Minerals in water
Chlorine or salt from pools
Saltwater exposure
Strong sun and heat
Frequent washing
How Skin Can Be Affected
Changes in skin are also common, particularly in the first week of travel.
You may notice:
Dryness or tightness after showering
Breakouts or congestion
Increased oil production
Sensitivity or mild irritation
This can happen due to:
Different water composition
Heat and humidity increasing sweat and oil
Sunscreen build-up
Frequent swimming and showering
Changes in diet and routine
This isn’t usually a sign of poor water quality — it’s your skin adjusting.
Pool Water & Its Effects
Most pools in Bali are treated with:
Chlorine, or
Saltwater systems (which still use sanitisation)
Pool water can:
Strip natural oils from hair and skin
Dry out hair, especially colour-treated hair
Cause dryness or irritation with frequent exposure
Saltwater pools are often gentler, but repeated swimming can still have a drying effect.
Practical Ways to Reduce Impact
For Hair
Rinse hair with fresh water before and after swimming
Use a leave-in conditioner or hair oil
Bring a purple or colour-protecting shampoo if you have blonde or dyed hair
Avoid washing hair daily if possible
Use a hat or protective style in strong sun
For Skin
Shower promptly after swimming
Use a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser
Moisturise regularly, especially after showers
Avoid over-exfoliating
Be mindful of sunscreen residue and cleanse thoroughly at night
Extra Tips for Sensitive Skin or Hair
Consider bringing familiar products from home
Patch-test new skincare products
Avoid switching multiple products at once
Drink plenty of water — hydration affects skin and hair more than people realise
Changes to skin and hair in Bali are common, temporary, and usually manageable. They’re caused by a combination of water differences, climate, and lifestyle changes — not something “wrong” with the water itself.
With a little preparation and simple adjustments, you can minimise dryness, irritation, and breakouts and enjoy your trip without worrying about your skin or hair.
🟢 IV Vitamin Drips (“Hangover Drips”)
IV vitamin drips are popular in Bali and are commonly used for hydration support, recovery after travel, heat exposure, or hangovers. Many providers are professional and well-reviewed, and some services even come to your accommodation. However, as with any medical-style service, the quality and hygiene standards can vary. The safest approach is to choose a reputable provider with strong reviews and clear clinical practices.
If choosing this:
Research providers thoroughly and read recent reviews
Look for clear hygiene practices and qualified staff
Avoid pop-up style services with no track record
Don’t proceed if anything feels rushed or unclear
If you have medical conditions or concerns, consult a medical professional first
🟢 Tattoos in Bali: What to Know Before You Book
Getting a tattoo in Bali is very common, and the island has a well-established tattoo scene. Many travellers choose to get tattooed here because of the range of skilled artists available and the fact that prices are often more affordable than in some other countries. As with anywhere in the world, however, quality, hygiene, and professionalism vary — so planning and research matter far more than price or convenience.
A tattoo is permanent. Treat the decision with the same care you would at home.
Choosing a Tattoo Studio
Where to Go
Reputable tattoo studios are most commonly found in:
Seminyak
Canggu
Kuta
Ubud
Sanur
These areas tend to have:
Established studios with strong reputations
Artists experienced with international clients
Clear hygiene procedures
English-speaking staff
Avoid choosing a studio simply because it’s nearby or has availability on the spot.
How to Choose a Reputable Studio
Before booking:
Read recent reviews, not just overall ratings
Look for photos of healed tattoos, not only fresh work
Review the artist’s portfolio and style
Check how long the studio has been operating
Avoid studios that pressure you into booking immediately
Good studios are happy for you to ask questions, take your time, and walk away if you’re unsure.
Price alone is not a reliable indicator of quality — expensive doesn’t always mean safer, and affordable doesn’t mean unsafe. Professional standards matter most.
Hygiene & Safety Standards (Non-Negotiable)
A reputable tattoo studio in Bali should follow strict hygiene procedures, including:
Single-use, disposable needles only
Needles opened from sealed packaging in front of you
Fresh gloves worn and changed regularly
Ink caps, razors, and wipes used once and discarded
Clean, organised workstations
Proper sharps and waste disposal
Many studios will proactively:
Show you the sealed needle
Confirm needle expiry dates
Explain their hygiene process without being asked
If a studio cannot clearly explain its hygiene practices, or something feels off, do not proceed. These are basic standards you should expect anywhere in the world.
Health Considerations Before Getting a Tattoo
Before your appointment:
Be well-rested and hydrated
Eat a proper meal
Avoid getting tattooed if you’re unwell, sunburnt, or have irritated skin
If you have:
Skin conditions
Allergies
Medical concerns
Questions about healing
Tell the artist beforehand so they can advise appropriately.
Drinking Alcohol While Getting a Tattoo
When you’re on holiday, having a drink can feel like part of the experience — and many travellers wonder whether drinking while getting a tattoo is okay.
In reality, many tattoo studios are fine with light alcohol consumption, such as a single beer or drink. However, alcohol and tattooing don’t always mix as smoothly as people expect.
Alcohol can:
Thin the blood, leading to increased bleeding
Make it harder for ink to settle cleanly
Increase swelling during and after the session
Affect healing in the days that follow
Excessive bleeding can also make detailed work more difficult for the artist and impact the final result.
Most reputable studios:
Are fine with minimal drinking
Will not tattoo someone who is visibly intoxicated
May refuse service if alcohol consumption affects safety or consent
This isn’t about judgement — it’s about quality, safety, and results.
A balanced approach:
Keep alcohol minimal or avoid it before your session
Stay hydrated
Eat beforehand
Avoid drinking heavily the night before
Many travellers choose to celebrate after their tattoo is finished rather than before.
Aftercare: What to Expect
Immediately After the Tattoo
Most professional studios will:
Clean the tattoo thoroughly
Apply an antibacterial ointment
Cover it with a protective dressing or “second skin”
Second skin is a medical-grade adhesive film that:
Protects the tattoo from bacteria
Reduces friction and contamination
Helps with early healing
Your artist should explain:
How long to keep it on
When and how to remove it
How to clean the tattoo afterward
Ongoing Aftercare (Your Responsibility)
Once you leave the studio, healing is your responsibility.
Typical aftercare includes:
Gently washing with mild, fragrance-free soap
Keeping the area clean and dry
Applying a recommended tattoo balm or moisturiser
Avoiding tight clothing over the tattoo
Not scratching, picking, or peeling
Studios may recommend:
A specific tattoo aftercare cream
A light, fragrance-free moisturiser
Gentle antibacterial soap
Follow the artist’s instructions closely — most healing issues come from poor aftercare, not the tattoo itself.
Swimming & Tattoos (Important)
Swimming is not recommended while a tattoo is healing.
This includes:
Pools
The ocean
Rivers
Hot tubs and spas
Why swimming should be avoided:
Fresh tattoos are open wounds
Water can introduce bacteria and increase infection risk
Salt and chlorine can irritate healing skin
Prolonged soaking can affect ink retention and healing
As a general guide:
Avoid swimming for at least two weeks
Larger or more detailed tattoos may require longer
If swimming is a major part of your trip, consider scheduling your tattoo toward the end of your stay.
Sun Exposure & Healing
Bali’s sun is strong, and fresh tattoos are especially sensitive.
While healing:
Keep tattoos out of direct sunlight
Do not apply sunscreen until the tattoo is fully healed
Cover with loose clothing when outdoors
Sun exposure during healing can cause fading, irritation, and uneven results.
Getting a tattoo in Bali can be a positive and memorable experience when done properly. The island has many skilled artists and professional studios, but the outcome depends on your preparation and choices.
Do your research, prioritise hygiene, be mindful with alcohol, follow aftercare instructions carefully, and plan around swimming and sun exposure. Treated with the same care you’d expect at home, getting a tattoo in Bali is no more risky than anywhere else in the world.
🟢 Manicures, Pedicures & Beauty Services in Bali: Hygiene & What to Know
Manicures, pedicures, and beauty treatments are extremely common in Bali and are one of the things many travellers love to include in their trip. From local salons to high-end spas, you can find a wide range of services — often at prices that feel very affordable compared to home.
If you enjoy these treatments, there’s no reason to skip them while you’re in Bali. Just approach them with the same awareness you would anywhere else in the world.
What to Expect
Depending on the salon or spa, you can find:
Basic walk-in nail salons
Boutique beauty studios
Luxury day spas and resort spas
Many places offer:
Manicures and pedicures
Gel and acrylic nails
Nail art
Facials, waxing, and other beauty treatments
Quality and pricing vary, but excellent services are widely available at many different price points.
Hygiene Matters (Everywhere)
As with beauty services anywhere in the world, hygiene is the most important thing to pay attention to.
Before starting a treatment, it’s reasonable to check that:
Tools are properly sanitised or taken from sealed packaging
Nail files, buffers, and pumice stones are clean or single-use
Foot basins and workstations look clean and well maintained
Staff wash or sanitise their hands and wear gloves where appropriate
Reputable salons are used to these expectations and won’t be offended by questions.
Things to Be Mindful Of
Avoid treatments if you have open cuts, broken skin, or infections
Be cautious with aggressive cuticle cutting or scraping
Let staff know if you have sensitive skin or allergies
If something doesn’t feel right, it’s okay to stop or leave
Infections, irritation, or reactions can happen anywhere — Bali is no different. Awareness, not fear, is what keeps you safe.
Choosing Where to Go
A few simple tips help reduce risk:
Read recent reviews
Look at photos of the space and tools
Choose places that appear clean, organised, and professional
Don’t base your choice on price alone
Affordable doesn’t automatically mean unsafe — and expensive doesn’t automatically mean better. Hygiene and professionalism matter more than the price tag.
Aftercare & Common Sense
After treatments:
Keep nails and skin clean
Avoid picking or biting cuticles
Moisturise regularly
Monitor for redness, swelling, or discomfort
If anything feels unusual after a treatment, seek medical or pharmacy advice promptly.
🟡 Drinking in Public & Legal Age in Bali
Drinking alcohol in Bali is common and generally relaxed, particularly in tourist areas. Understanding how it works helps set expectations and avoid confusion.
Drinking in Public
In Bali, drinking alcohol in public is generally tolerated, especially in areas frequented by travellers. It’s common to see people:
Having a beer on the beach
Drinking at sunset spots
Carrying drinks while walking between venues
This is not unusual or frowned upon in most tourist areas. Alcohol is widely sold at:
Convenience stores
Supermarkets
Bars, cafés, and restaurants
As with many destinations, the atmosphere is casual rather than restrictive, particularly in Bali’s main travel hubs.
Legal Drinking Age (Factual)
Indonesia’s legal drinking age is 21.
That is the official law.
In practice, enforcement can vary. In many areas:
ID is not always requested
Age checks may be inconsistent
Under-21s may be able to purchase alcohol without proof
This is relatively common and often overlooked, particularly in tourist-heavy locations. That said, the legal age itself does not change — enforcement simply varies.
A Note on Littering
Please:
Dispose of bottles and cans in bins
Don’t leave rubbish on beaches, streets, or public areas
Where possible:
Give refundable bottles or cans to a local
Many locals collect recyclables as part of their income
This small action makes a meaningful difference and shows respect for the island and its communities.
🟢 Fireworks in Bali: What to Know
Fireworks are something travellers often notice in Bali, particularly around celebrations, events, and certain times of year. While they are available, there are some important practical and legal points worth understanding.
Purchasing Fireworks
Fireworks can be purchased in Bali, most commonly:
From roadside stalls during festive periods
Near major holidays and celebrations
Occasionally from local markets or temporary vendors
Availability is seasonal and usually increases around major events such as New Year’s Eve or local celebrations. Fireworks are not always sold year-round in convenience stores.
Rules & Legal Context
In Indonesia, fireworks are regulated, and their use is technically restricted. The rules are designed to manage safety, noise, and public order.
In practice:
Small consumer fireworks are commonly used during celebrations
Enforcement varies depending on location, timing, and scale
Larger or more powerful fireworks are more likely to attract attention from authorities
Fireworks are not permitted in certain situations, particularly:
Near airports or flight paths
In crowded public spaces
In areas where they pose a fire or safety risk
Where Fireworks Are (and Aren’t) Appropriate
Fireworks are more commonly seen:
During organised events
At private celebrations where permission has been granted
In open areas away from crowds
They should not be used:
On public roads
In busy streets or markets
In protected natural areas
Near temples or during religious ceremonies
Villas, Hotels & Private Property
If you’re staying in a villa or hotel:
Always ask permission before using fireworks
Many properties prohibit them entirely due to fire risk and insurance restrictions
Some villas may allow small fireworks for special occasions if approved in advance
Property owners and managers have the final say, regardless of where the fireworks were purchased.
Noise & Community Considerations
Bali is a residential island with strong community ties. Even when fireworks are tolerated:
Late-night use may not be welcomed
Noise affects families, ceremonies, and animals
What feels like a private celebration may impact neighbours
Checking first is always the safest and most respectful approach.
🔴 Medical Care in Bali
Bali has reputable medical clinics and international-standard hospitals in key areas, and many doctors speak English. Medical care is accessible, but the standard can vary depending on location and the facility you choose. It’s always better to seek help early rather than waiting until something becomes worse. Your travel insurance is crucial here — not only for serious events, but also for peace of mind if you need IV fluids, medications, or clinic visits.
Important reminders:
Have travel insurance that covers your activities (especially scooters/adventure sports)
Keep receipts and medical paperwork for claims
Don’t delay treatment — early intervention is usually simpler and cheaper

🟢 Islands Around Bali
Beyond mainland Bali, there are several stunning nearby islands that are well worth considering as part of your trip. Each offers a slightly different pace, landscape, and experience — from relaxed beach towns to dramatic coastlines and crystal-clear waters.
These islands are easy to access and can be visited as a day trip or overnight stay, depending on how much time you have.

🟢 Languages in Bali
Bali is multilingual, which can sometimes feel confusing to visitors — especially when you hear different languages used in different situations.
The most important thing to know is this: you do not need to speak multiple languages to travel comfortably in Bali.
This section is included for context and reassurance only.
The Languages You’ll Encounter
Balinese
Balinese is the traditional local language used within families, communities, and religious or cultural settings. It’s closely tied to Balinese identity and ceremony.
You are not expected to speak Balinese as a visitor.
You may hear or see the greeting “Om Swastiastu”, which is a respectful traditional greeting commonly used at the start of conversations, ceremonies, or formal interactions. Using it is appreciated, but never required.
You may also hear “suksma”, which means thank you in Balinese. This is a polite, traditional expression that is warmly received, but completely optional for visitors.
Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia)
Indonesian is the national language of Indonesia and is used for everyday communication across the country.
Most Balinese people are bilingual or multilingual and will often switch to Indonesian when speaking with visitors or in professional settings.
If you choose to use a local phrase, Indonesian is the most practical and widely used option.
English
English is widely spoken in tourist areas, including hotels, restaurants, transport services, tour operators, and shops.
Most travellers will navigate Bali entirely in English without any difficulty.
Polite Phrases (Optional)
If you’d like to use a greeting or say thank you, these are simple and appropriate:
Hello (formal / respectful): Om Swastiastu
Hello (casual): Halo
Thank you (Indonesian): Terima kasih
Thank you (Balinese): Suksma
Using any of these is polite. Using none of them is also completely fine.
A Simple Rule of Thumb
If you’re unsure which language to use, English is perfectly acceptable.
If you’d like to show extra courtesy, a simple greeting or thank you is enough. There is no expectation to perform cultural fluency — awareness and respect matter far more than pronunciation.

🟡 Local Government, Community Authority & Police in Bali
Understanding how authority and law enforcement work in Bali is important for visitors. Bali operates with both formal government systems and traditional community governance, and knowing the difference can help you navigate issues smoothly and respectfully.
🟡 Banjar – Bali’s Local Community Authority
A Banjar is the smallest and most influential local community unit in Bali. Every neighbourhood belongs to a Banjar, and they play a central role in daily life.
What does a Banjar do?
Banjar councils are responsible for:
Local community rules (adat or customary law)
Neighbourhood harmony and dispute resolution
Cultural and religious ceremonies
Managing local events and street access
Community-based security (often through pecalang, traditional security guards)
Banjar authorities are not police, but they are highly respected. In many situations—especially minor issues such as misunderstandings, noise complaints, local access concerns, or community-related matters—Banjar leaders are often the first point of contact and may help resolve issues quickly without escalation.
In tourist areas such as Legian, Kuta, Seminyak, and Ubud, Banjar members may work alongside accommodation providers or police when tourists are involved.
🟡 Traditional Security – Pecalang
Pecalang are traditional Balinese community security officers appointed by the Banjar. You’ll often see them during:
Religious ceremonies
Festivals
Traffic diversions
Community events
They wear traditional attire (often black-and-white checkered cloth) and help manage order locally. While they do not have police authority, they can:
Direct traffic
Control access to streets
Assist in resolving local matters
Coordinate with police if necessary
They should always be treated with respect.
🟡 Police in Bali – Indonesian National Police (POLRI)
Formal law enforcement in Bali is handled by the Indonesian National Police (POLRI).
What the police handle
Police are responsible for:
Crime and criminal investigations
Emergencies and public safety
Traffic accidents and road incidents
Theft, assault, and disputes
Lost or stolen passports
Legal reports and official documentation
The regional police authority for Bali is Polda Bali, which oversees all local stations across the island.
🟡 Tourist Police
Bali has dedicated Tourist Police units in major visitor areas. They are specifically trained to assist foreign visitors and are generally more comfortable communicating in English.
Tourist Police can help with:
Lost passports or belongings
Scams or disputes involving tourists
Safety advice
Directing you to the correct police station or authority
Availability and numbers may change, but accommodation staff can usually help contact tourist police if needed.

🟡 Natural Disaster Awareness: Earthquakes & Tsunamis
Bali is part of the Pacific Ring of Fire, a geological zone where tectonic plates interact and seismic activity is more common. That does not mean it’s dangerous for visitors — but it does mean being aware is part of informed travel. i
Earthquakes in Bali
Small to moderate earthquakes can occur fairly regularly around Bali. Most go unnoticed or cause minimal shaking. These are part of natural plate movement and Indonesian scientific agencies monitor seismic activity closely.
Earthquakes of significant strength are rare and usually occur offshore or away from populated areas. Even when shaking is felt, Indonesian disaster management systems and building practices help ensure that severe damage is uncommon.
Tsunami Risk
Tsunamis are less common than earthquakes, but because Bali is on an island and surrounded by ocean, they are a scientifically recognised risk — not a guaranteed event. Most tsunamis originate from large offshore earthquakes, and authorities have warning systems and evacuation plans in place. Bali has early warning sirens in many coastal areas and clear evacuation routes. FINNS Beach Club
The key is preparedness, not panic.
What to do if you feel a strong quake:
If outdoors near the coast, move inland and uphill
Stay away from beaches until authorities confirm safety
Follow local emergency announcements
Know evacuation points for your accommodation area
Being informed gives you perspective — Bali has hosted millions of travellers safely despite its natural setting.

🟡 Visas & Overstaying in Bali — What You Need to Know
Indonesia (including Bali) has a structured and enforceable immigration system that applies to all visitors. Bali is part of Indonesia, and the rules you follow here are the same as elsewhere in the country — no matter how relaxed parts of the island may feel in everyday life.

🔴 Return Forms & Simple Prep
Depending on your country of citizenship and entry requirements, you may be required to complete a return or arrival declaration form for your home country. These forms will be handed to you when you are on the plane.
These forms are sometimes:
Provided on the plane
Completed digitally before landing
Required only for certain nationalities
To make this easy:
Carry a pen in your carry-on
Keep a clear photo of your passport on your phone
This saves time and avoids the awkward wait for someone nearby to finish using their pen.
🟡 Declaring Items When You Return Home
When leaving Bali, most travellers don’t need to complete any special exit declarations.
However, you may be required to declare certain items when you arrive back in your home country, depending on that country’s customs rules.
This is not specific to Bali — it applies to international travel anywhere.
What This Usually Applies To
Depending on your country, you may need to declare:
alcohol or tobacco over allowance limits
high-value purchases (such as jewellery, electronics, or luxury items)
commercial goods or items intended for resale
large amounts of cash
restricted or controlled items
Souvenirs and everyday personal purchases are usually fine, but limits vary by country.
What You Should Do
Check your home country’s customs website before returning
Be honest when completing arrival cards or digital declarations
Keep receipts for higher-value purchases
If unsure, declare — customs officers can advise
Declaring an item does not automatically mean you’ll be charged. Failing to declare something that should have been declared can cause issues.
A Simple Rule of Thumb
Bali doesn’t control what you declare when you get home — your country does.
If you’re unsure whether something needs to be declared, it’s always safer to ask on arrival.
🟢 Returning Home — Reverse Culture Shock & Recovery
A Bali trip often feels intense in the best ways — rich sensory experiences, new rhythms, new routines. But when you return home, it’s common to experience a subtle kind of adjustment called reverse culture shock.
You may notice:
Feeling more tired than usual
A desire for quiet or simplicity
Slower re-entry into your regular routine
Heightened appreciation for new perspectives
This is normal. Giving yourself a day or two at home to rest, reflect, and decompress helps your body and mind re-settle. Many travellers find that the biggest impact of a Bali trip is internal and long-lasting
Part 13: Bali Hacks and Tips
Reference Guide: 🟢
🟢 Your best tools when travelling in Bali are simple ones: Google, a bit of research, social media, and reading recent reviews. These resources will answer most questions before they become problems and help you make more confident decisions.
The tips below are small things you can choose to implement to further enhance your trip. They’re not rules, and there’s no right or wrong way to travel — just practical insights designed to help you understand parts of Bali travel you may not realise exist until you’re already there.
Health & Body Hacks (Start Here)
Pocari Sweat is an electrolyte drink locals swear by — great for heat, dehydration, hangovers, or feeling flat.
Teh Kotak (the red-and-white boxed tea) is gentle on the stomach and often used when people feel a bit off.
Cold Stop is an over-the-counter cold and flu medication that works well if you feel run down or congested.
Ondansetron can be purchased over the counter and is excellent for nausea and vomiting.
Loperamide is available without a prescription and helps manage diarrhoea when needed.
Diapet diarrhoea tablets are sold in convenience stores and work surprisingly well.
Norit (activated charcoal) helps absorb toxins — don’t take it alongside other medication, and be consistent if using it.
Antibiotics: if prescribed, always finish the full course, even if you feel better — Bali belly can return if you stop early.
Daily vitamins can help support immunity — start taking them about 7 days before you leave.
Electrolytes with breakfast help you feel more hydrated and energised for the day.
Water before bed (if you can manage it) helps you wake up feeling better in the heat.
Sea sickness tablets are worth having if you’re going on a boat — even if you don’t usually get sick.
24/7 pharmacists can be contacted via WhatsApp and will diagnose and deliver straight to your villa.
Bali belly doctors can come to your accommodation and may return if symptoms persist — call them again if needed.
Food, Drinks & Comfort
You can hire eskies (coolers) if you like your drinks very cold — handy for villas and beach days.
Ice is sold at convenience stores — confirm it’s drinkable if you want full control.
Cold drinks tip: some bars will freeze bottled drinks or provide ice buckets if you ask.
Private chefs can come to your villa, cook everything, and you just pay for groceries plus their service fee.
Alcohol bought from reputable supermarkets is safe — spirits often cost similar to Australia, so duty free isn’t essential.
Wine is expensive in Bali due to import taxes, which is why prices are higher than many expect.
Transport & Getting Around
Busy periods can turn short trips long — a 10-minute drive can become 1.5 hours in peak traffic.
If timing matters, stay closer to where you plan to spend time or travel earlier in the day.
Drivers hired for the day will wait patiently while you explore — tipping is appreciated but not required.
Small cash helps for transport, tips, and quick transactions.
Accommodation & Villa Life
Villa staff WhatsApp groups are a game-changer — ask for one so you can message directly if needed.
Villa services are convenient but can be marked up — you’re free to organise your own.
Tipping villa staff often leads to extra help like picking up food or drinks.
Laundry is easy and cheap — many places charge per kilo and offer pickup and delivery.
Do your laundry before flying home — unpacking is easier and everything smells amazing.
Decorations for birthdays or celebrations can be arranged through your accommodation or third parties.
Bartenders can come to your villa and may even supply alcohol.
24/7 Cleaning Services – If you need a cleaner outside standard hours and your villa doesn’t offer round-the-clock housekeeping, there are independent 24/7 cleaning services available that can come directly to your accommodation, even late at night.
Beauty, Wellness & Lifestyle
Facials and hair care: skip generic massage parlours if you’re serious — look for specialised skin or hair studios.
Gym and fitness passes are often available as casual visits — research, read reviews, and message them first.
Hair wash only services are cheap and great if you didn’t pack shampoo and conditioner.
Jewellery can change colour due to water and humidity — consider leaving valuable pieces at home.
Shopping, Money & Practical Tips
Locals can help you save money — relationships matter and locals often know better prices.
Use social media to find services, check real work, and message businesses directly.
Join Bali traveller Facebook groups — they’re full of real reviews and honest recommendations.
ATMs don’t always work — try another if one fails, especially in convenience stores.
Exchange money when rates are good if you travel often — travel cards make this easy.
Carry small notes for tips, tolls, or quick purchases.
Return home with rupiah if you plan to come back — it saves hassle next trip.
Tech & Daily Carry
Power banks are essential — photos, videos, and maps drain phones fast.
Charge your power bank daily so it’s always ready.
Power cords and adapters are handy — cafés and bars will often charge your phone for you.
Portable speakers are worth bringing for villas if no Bluetooth speaker is provided.
Sleep masks help — not all rooms block light fully.
Gojek Hacks
Gojek can deliver almost anything, not just food — you can estimate the price in advance.
Adding a tip note increases the chance a driver accepts your order.
Food delivery is excellent — read reviews before ordering.
Leave clear delivery notes so drivers know where to drop food.
You can order two Gojeks at once, then order more after they’re completed.
Gojek cars are reliable and affordable, similar to Blue Bird taxis.
Social & Cultural Awareness
Beach vendors: if you engage one, others may follow — fun for some, tiring for others.
Begging children are part of organised family operations — it’s okay not to give cash.
Tipping goes a long way — small amounts make a big difference, especially for repeat services.
Street animals: don’t feed leftovers without knowing what’s safe for them.
Final Practical Bits
Carry a pen when flying — you may need it for arrival or return forms.
Keep a clear photo of your passport on your phone for easy access.
Book popular restaurants ahead via WhatsApp or their website.
Designer “genuine fake” items are often found in boutiques, not on the street — research first.
Villa shopping services can bring products to you — ask if they have what you want.
Fixed price stores aren’t always fixed — polite negotiation can still work.
Fake perfumes often don’t last long — manage expectations.
